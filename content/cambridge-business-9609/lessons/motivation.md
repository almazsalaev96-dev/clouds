## 30 seconds

Motivation theories are models of *why people work*, and each one implies a different management action. Taylor says pay for output; Maslow says needs form a ladder; Herzberg says the things that stop dissatisfaction are not the things that motivate. The exam skill is choosing the right model for the case and knowing where it fails.

## Standard

**Taylor (scientific management).** Workers are rational income-maximisers, so pay piece-rates and design work efficiently. It raises output for simple, measurable tasks — and treats people as machines, which is exactly where it breaks.

**Mayo (human relations).** The Hawthorne studies suggested attention, group dynamics and being consulted move output more than physical conditions do. The lesson: social needs are productive facts, not soft extras.

**Maslow (hierarchy of needs).** Physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualisation. A need only motivates while unmet, and higher levels only matter once lower ones are broadly satisfied. Useful as a diagnostic: *which* level is this workforce actually on?

**Herzberg (two-factor).** Hygiene factors — pay, conditions, supervision, security — cause dissatisfaction when bad but do not motivate when good. Motivators — achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement — are what actually drive effort. Implication: fixing pay stops the bleeding; job enrichment starts the engine.

**McGregor (Theory X / Theory Y).** Not two types of worker — two sets of *assumptions held by managers*, each self-fulfilling: controlled workers behave as if they need control.

## Exam level

The marks are almost never for reciting a theory; they are for **using one to explain this case**. The pattern that scores: name the theory in a clause, apply its mechanism to a specific detail (the 34% turnover, the removed bonus, the delegated decision), and develop the consequence for this business.

Financial methods (piece rate, commission, bonus, profit share) map to Taylor-style assumptions; non-financial methods (job enrichment, delegation, teamworking, empowerment) map to Herzberg/Maslow. Strong answers say which fits *this* workforce, and why.

**Most commonly lost here:** treating "motivated staff work harder" as analysis. It is an assertion — the chain must show *which need is met, by what mechanism, with what measurable consequence*.

## Misconceptions

- Pay is a motivator in Herzberg's model → Pay is a hygiene factor: poor pay demotivates, but good pay does not by itself create motivation.
- Maslow's hierarchy applies identically to everyone → The order and importance of needs vary by person and culture; the model is a diagnostic, not a law.
- Theory X and Theory Y describe types of worker → They describe managers' assumptions, which then shape worker behaviour.

## Limitations

- Most of the evidence base is old, Western and industrial; transfer to modern service workforces is partial.
- Maslow's levels are hard to observe or measure, and people pursue several at once.
- Herzberg's findings rely on self-reported critical incidents, which flatter respondents.
- Taylor's piece-rates fail wherever quality matters more than volume, or output is hard to attribute to one person.

## Key terms

- **Hygiene factor** — A job aspect whose absence causes dissatisfaction but whose presence does not motivate.
- **Motivator** — A job aspect that positively drives effort: achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement.
- **Job enrichment** — Redesigning work to add responsibility and meaning, not merely more tasks.
- **Piece rate** — Payment per unit produced.
