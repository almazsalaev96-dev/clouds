## 30 seconds

Break-even output is the number of units at which total revenue exactly covers total cost. You find it by asking how many units of contribution are needed to pay off the fixed costs: fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit. Every unit after that point adds its contribution straight to profit.

## Simple

Every unit you sell brings in money, and every unit costs something to make. The difference between those two — price minus variable cost — is called **contribution**, because that is what each unit contributes towards paying the bills that exist whether you sell anything or not: rent, salaries, insurance.

So the question "how many do I need to sell?" becomes "how many contributions do I need to cover the fixed bills?"

If rent and salaries come to $80,000 a year, and each unit contributes $10, you need 8,000 units. Sell 8,001 and you are $10 in profit. Sell 7,999 and you are $10 down.

## Standard

Break-even analysis rests on separating costs by behaviour rather than by type. Fixed costs do not change with output in the short run; variable costs change in direct proportion to it. Contribution per unit is selling price minus variable cost per unit, and break-even output is fixed costs divided by contribution per unit.

The **margin of safety** is the gap between current output and break-even output — how much demand could fall before the business makes a loss. Expressed as a percentage of current output, it is directly comparable between firms, which is what makes it more useful than the raw break-even figure.

Three levers move break-even, and they move it by different amounts:

- **Raising price** increases contribution per unit, lowering break-even — but usually reduces volume, and by how much depends on price elasticity.
- **Cutting variable cost** increases contribution per unit with no demand penalty, which is why it is generally the first lever to reach for.
- **Cutting fixed cost** lowers the numerator directly, but fixed costs are typically the hardest to change quickly.

## Exam level

Break-even appears in two shapes, and they reward different things.

**Calculation questions** are AO1/AO2 and are effectively free marks once the formula is automatic. Show contribution as a separate line, state the unit, and add one sentence saying what the number means — that interpreting sentence is frequently where the final mark sits.

**Analysis and evaluation questions** almost never ask you to compute break-even. They give you the figure, or two figures, and ask what follows. The marks are in the chain, not the arithmetic: a low margin of safety *means* the firm cannot absorb a demand shock, *which means* it has no room to cut price competitively, *which means* a rival with a wider margin can start a price war it cannot answer.

The limitations are the AO4 bank, and they are worth learning as sentences rather than as a list:

- It assumes all output is sold, which is exactly the assumption that fails in a downturn.
- It assumes costs behave linearly, but bulk discounts and overtime premiums both break that.
- It assumes a single product or a constant sales mix, so for a multi-product firm the figure is an aggregate that may describe none of the products.
- It is a snapshot: the moment any input price moves, the answer moves.

**Most commonly lost here:** computing the number correctly and then saying nothing about what it means for the business in question.

## Misconceptions

- Break-even is the point where the business starts making money on each unit → Every unit makes a contribution from the first one. Break-even is where cumulative contribution has covered fixed costs.
- Contribution is profit → Contribution is what is left to pay fixed costs. It only becomes profit once fixed costs are covered.
- A lower break-even is always better → It is better all else equal, but a business can lower break-even by cutting fixed costs in ways that damage capacity or quality.

## Limitations

- Assumes everything produced is sold.
- Assumes fixed and variable costs are cleanly separable and behave linearly.
- Assumes a constant sales mix in multi-product firms.
- Ignores the time value of money and is a single-period snapshot.
- Says nothing about whether the required output is achievable given market size.

## Key terms

- **Contribution per unit** — Selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit.
- **Break-even output** — The output at which total revenue equals total cost.
- **Margin of safety** — The amount by which current output exceeds break-even output.
- **Fixed cost** — A cost that does not vary with output in the short run.
- **Variable cost** — A cost that varies directly with output.
