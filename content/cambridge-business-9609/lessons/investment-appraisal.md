      - Averaging the inflows instead of accumulating them year by year.
  - name: Average rate of return (ARR)
    expression: (average annual profit ÷ initial investment) × 100
    commonMistakes:
      - Using cash inflows instead of profit (profit = inflows − depreciation of the outlay).
      - Forgetting to ×100.
  - name: Net present value (NPV)
    expression: sum of (net cash flow × discount factor) − initial outlay
    commonMistakes:
      - Discounting the initial outlay (it is already at present value).
      - Misreading the discount table's rate or year.

## 30 seconds

Three tools for one question — is this investment worth it? Payback measures *how fast the money comes back*, ARR measures *the average return*, NPV measures *value in today's money*. They can disagree about the same project, and the exam's favourite question is what to do when they do.

## Standard

**Payback** builds a cumulative cash column and finds where it crosses the outlay. Simple, cash-focused, and biased toward fast projects; it ignores everything after payback and the time value of money.

**ARR** compares average annual *profit* (not cash) to the outlay, giving a percentage comparable to interest rates and ROCE. It uses the whole project life but ignores *when* money arrives — $1m in year one and $1m in year nine count the same.

**NPV** fixes exactly that: each year's cash flow is multiplied by a discount factor that shrinks with time, then summed against the outlay. Positive NPV means the project beats the discount rate. It is the most complete method — and the most dependent on two guesses: the forecast cash flows and the chosen rate.

Qualitative factors sit alongside all three: fit with corporate objectives, workforce and community impact, risk appetite, reversibility.

## Exam level

Calculations are AO1/AO2 marks that must be automatic: cumulative column for payback, the depreciation adjustment for ARR, careful table-reading for NPV, units and one interpreting sentence every time.

The evaluation marks live in **method choice as a function of context**: a cash-constrained firm rationally weights payback; a technology market makes distant forecasts near-fiction, weakening NPV's advantage; a stable utility with cheap capital should trust NPV. A committed judgement about *which method should lead for this firm, and under what condition that would change* is the top-band move.

**Most commonly lost here:** presenting all three results and concluding "the firm should consider all methods". That is a list, not a judgement, and it leaves the AO4 marks on the table.

## Misconceptions

- ARR uses cash inflows → ARR uses profit: subtract the depreciation of the outlay from total inflows before averaging.
- A positive NPV guarantees the project succeeds → It says the *forecast* beats the discount rate; the forecast is the weakest link.
- Shortest payback is always best → Payback ignores everything that happens after it; a fast-payback project can be the least profitable on the table.

## Limitations

- All three depend entirely on forecast cash flows, which degrade with distance.
- None of the numbers capture strategic value, risk profile, or reversibility.
- The discount rate is a choice, and changing it can flip an NPV's sign.

## Key terms

- **Discount factor** — The multiplier that converts a future cash flow into today's value at a given rate.
- **Time value of money** — A dollar now is worth more than a dollar later, because it can be invested and because the future is uncertain.
- **Opportunity cost of capital** — The return the money could earn elsewhere; the floor an investment must beat.
