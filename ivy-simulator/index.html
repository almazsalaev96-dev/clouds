Read this before you trust a number. No public dataset exists of real applications with their outcomes — admissions files are confidential and nothing was trained on them. What this docket is built on is the material that is genuinely public: the 1–6 reader rubric and the admit-rate-by-rating figures disclosed in SFFA v. Harvard; each school's Common Data Set, which publishes both its admit rate and which factors it rates “very important”; and the score and rank distributions of enrolled students. So treat the output as a well-calibrated second opinion, not a prediction. The ratings and the coaching are where the value is. The percentage is the least reliable line on the page.
I

The file

Which school, and which round. Round matters more than most applicants think, and for a different reason than they think — see the report.

II

Academic record

This section decides whether the rest of the file gets read carefully. It almost never decides the admit. Roughly three-quarters of the applicants at these schools are academically qualified; the academic rating is a gate, not a race.

III

Activities

Enter your strongest few, not all ten. The rating is set by your best one or two — a long list of tier-4 memberships scores the same as a short one. Choose the tier honestly; almost everyone over-rates themselves by one level.

"Well-rounded" is what the class is, not what a member of it is.
IV

The personal statement

Paste the full essay. The diagnostic below is mechanical — it measures concreteness, cliché load, structure, and voice, which is what most weak drafts fail on. It cannot judge whether the essay is true or moving. For that, use the deep-read prompt at the bottom of the report.

V

School support

Letters are rated separately from everything else and they matter more than applicants expect. A letter that says "one of the two best I have taught in twenty years" is a different document from a warm one.

VI

Context and institutional factors

Every file is read against the opportunity available to it. The same activity list means something different at a school that sends forty students a year to these colleges and one that has never sent any. The second group below is the part of admissions nobody enjoys discussing; the docket includes it because pretending it does not exist produces bad advice.
