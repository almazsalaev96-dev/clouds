# Ivy Admissions — how the reading actually works, and how the simulator models it

> Companion to `index.html` (**The Reader's Docket**). Read this once, use the tool
> repeatedly.

---

## PART 0 — The honest bit, first

You asked for a simulator trained on five years of accepted, waitlisted and rejected
applications. **That dataset does not exist and cannot be built.** Admissions files are
confidential; no school releases applications with their outcomes; nothing public has ever
been trained on them. Any site claiming otherwise is either using self-reported forum data
(wildly biased — people report acceptances far more than rejections) or making it up.

So here is what the docket is actually built on, all of it public and checkable:

| Source | What it gives | How reliable |
|---|---|---|
| **SFFA v. Harvard** trial record (2018–2023) | The real 1–6 reader rubric, the real rating definitions, and admit rates **by rating** and **by hook** | Very — sworn testimony and expert reports on ~160,000 applications |
| **Common Data Set**, Section C7 | Each school's own published statement of which factors it rates *very important / important / considered* | Very — schools publish it themselves |
| **Common Data Set**, C1/C2/C9/C11 | Admit rates, waitlist numbers, score and rank distributions of enrolled students | Very |
| Public admit-rate announcements | Per-cycle rates, where the school still announces them | Good, but several Ivies stopped announcing after 2021 |

Everything the tool tells you is derived from those. **The ratings and the coaching are the
product. The percentage is the weakest line on the page** — treat it as an order of
magnitude, not a forecast.

## PART 1 — The single most important fact

**Roughly three-quarters of applicants to these schools are academically qualified.**

Harvard's own filings showed that a large majority of its applicants could do the work.
The academic rating is therefore a **gate**, not a race. Once you are through it, more
academic perfection buys you almost nothing.

This has a brutal corollary, and it is the thing most students get wrong:

> A 4.0 and a 1580 do not make you competitive. They make you **readable**.
> What makes you competitive is the extracurricular and personal ratings.

The docket encodes this literally: a flawless transcript with no scholarship beyond
coursework is capped at academic **2**, and a file with perfect academics and only
school-level activities comes out around **2%** at Harvard. That is not the tool being
harsh. That is the actual shape of the pool.

## PART 2 — The rubric you are actually being scored on

Every file gets these ratings from the first reader. **1 is best, 6 is worst.** Readers add
`+` and `-` modifiers.

### Academic

| | What it means |
|---|---|
| **1** | Summa potential. Near-perfect record **plus original scholarship** — published research, national olympiad medal, mathematics well past the curriculum |
| **2** | Magna potential. Excellent grades, top scores, hardest curriculum available. **This is the realistic ceiling for almost everyone, and it is enough** |
| **3** | Cum laude potential. Very good student, scores roughly 1400–1490 |
| **4** | Adequate |
| **5–6** | Below the threshold |

### Extracurricular — *the rating that decides unhooked files*

| | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | National or international achievement, or professional-standard work | National champion or medallist, published author, founder of something with genuine outside reach, national office |
| **2** | Recognition **beyond your own school** | State award, regional office, an organisation whose impact can be counted, all-state musician |
| **3** | Real responsibility, school-bounded | Club president, team captain, editor-in-chief |
| **4** | Participation | Member. Volunteer hours. |

The line that matters is **between 3 and 2, and it is the school gate**. Everything inside
your school is a 3. Recognition outside it is a 2. This is why "president of four clubs"
scores the same as "president of one" — and why entering one state competition can be worth
more than a year of meetings.

### Personal

Character, as it reaches the reader through essays, supplements and interview. This is where
your essay lands. Note: since *SFFA* (2023) **race cannot be a factor**, though an essay may
still discuss how race has affected your life, and readers weigh what you did with the
circumstances you had.

### School support

The counsellor and teacher letters, rated on their own. **The most underrated line in the
whole file** and the cheapest to improve — see Part 5.

### Overall

Not an average. A holistic judgement in which a strong **spike** outweighs a flat set of
good ratings. From the trial record, roughly:

| Overall rating | Admit rate |
|---|---|
| 1 | Essentially all |
| 2 | Around half |
| 3 | Single digits |
| 4 or worse | Near zero |

**A "2" overall is the realistic target.** A "3" is where most strong applicants land, and
"3" is a denial at these admit rates.

## PART 3 — The parts nobody enjoys discussing

From the Harvard trial record, admit rates by category:

| Category | Admit rate |
|---|---|
| Recruited athletes | ~86% |
| Legacies | ~33% |
| Dean's / director's interest list | Very high |
| **Everyone else** | **~4.5%** |

Two honest readings of this:

1. **If you have one of these, use it.** Apply early, contact the coach, tick the box.
2. **If you do not, stop comparing yourself to people who do.** A meaningful share of every
   admitted class is decided by things you cannot change. The competition for the remaining
   places is the only competition you are in.

The docket applies these as multipliers **deliberately smaller than the raw ratios above**,
because part of any such ratio is that hooked applicants also tend to be strong applicants.

**On Early Decision:** the ED rate is often two to three times the regular rate, and the
naive reading — "apply early for a boost" — is mostly wrong. Much of that gap is athletes
and legacies concentrated in the early pool. The real, honest gains are a smaller pool and
demonstrated commitment. It is worth doing **only if the school is genuinely your first
choice**, because it is binding.

**On being international:** admit rates for international applicants run roughly 2–4%, and
several of these schools are *need-aware* for internationals, meaning asking for aid is
itself a negative factor. Harvard, Yale, Princeton and MIT are need-blind for international
applicants; most of the rest are not. This is the single largest structural factor in the
model for a non-US applicant, and it is why applying to a wider range of schools matters more
for you than for a domestic applicant.

## PART 4 — The essay

### What the diagnostic in the tool measures

Mechanical things only — but they are the things a reader notices in the first fifteen
seconds, and they are where nearly every weak draft fails:

- **Concreteness**: names, numbers, physical detail per 100 words. Under 2.4 reads abstract.
- **Abstraction load**: *passion, journey, resilience, impact*. Over 3.2 per 100 words and
  you are telling, not showing.
- **Clichés**: ~40 stock admissions phrases. Every one has been read several hundred times
  this week.
- **Opening**: definitions, famous quotes and "ever since I was young" are the three worst
  openings in the genre.
- **Sentence-length variance**: flat rhythm reads as flat voice.
- **Reflection in the final third**: does the essay *turn*, or just narrate?

### The five rules that matter more than any of that

1. **Write about something small.** One afternoon, not one lifetime. The essay is 650 words;
   a whole childhood does not fit and always flattens.
2. **Show one scene a camera could have recorded**, then think about it. Most weak essays are
   90% reflection and 10% scene. Invert it.
3. **The turn is the point.** You thought one thing. By the end you think something more
   complicated. An essay without a turn is a diary entry.
4. **Cut the moral.** "This experience taught me…" hands the reader a lesson they would
   rather find themselves. End on the concrete.
5. **No topic is banned, but some are crowded** — the service trip, the sports injury, the
   grandparent's death, the immigration story, the pandemic. If you write one, the specificity
   has to carry it entirely.

### The supplements are not optional

A generic supplement is a **net negative**, not a neutral. Name two real courses, one real
professor, and one thing at that school you could not get anywhere else. A reader can spot a
recycled supplement in one sentence, and it reads as indifference.

## PART 5 — The cheapest wins available to you

Ranked by return on effort:

1. **Fix the supplements.** Hours of work. Turns a negative into a positive.
2. **Cultivate one superlative letter.** Choose the teacher who watched you struggle and come
   back — not the one who gave you the easiest A. Ask early, in person, and hand them a page
   about what you actually did in their class. The gap between a warm letter and a striking
   one is worth more than a hundred SAT points.
3. **Book a real meeting with your counsellor.** A counsellor who barely knows you writes a
   form letter. One hour fixes it.
4. **Rewrite the personal statement.** Weeks, not years — and it is the only part of the file
   you can still change completely.
5. **Push one activity outside your school.** Enter it, publish it, take it to real users, run
   for the regional office. This is the 3 → 2 jump, and it is the highest-value thing on this
   list if you still have a year.
6. **Retest only if you are below the school's 25th percentile.** Above it, more points buy
   nothing.

Note what is *not* on this list: joining more clubs, taking a twelfth AP, or raising a 1540
to a 1570.

## PART 6 — Using the tool honestly

- **Rate your activities one tier lower than you want to.** Almost everyone over-rates
  themselves by exactly one level. If you are unsure between two tiers, pick the lower one —
  the report is more useful when the input is pessimistic.
- **Run it for several schools.** The same file is a different application at Harvard and at
  Cornell. Watch what changes and what does not.
- **Watch the ratings, not the percentage.** Moving Personal from 3 to 2 is real information.
  Moving 9% to 12% is noise.
- **Use the deep-read prompt.** The diagnostic counts things; it cannot tell you whether your
  essay sounds like you. Copy the generated block into Claude and get an actual read.

### Verify before you rely on it

Admit rates and score ranges change every year, and several schools stopped publishing.
Search `"<school name> common data set"`, open the current year's PDF, and check:

- **C1/C2** — applicants, admits, waitlist offered and accepted
- **C7** — which factors the school itself rates *very important*
- **C9** — enrolled score ranges

If a number in the tool disagrees with the school's own CDS, **the CDS is right**.

---

## PART 7 — The last thing

At a 3.6% admit rate, a denial is not a verdict on you. Harvard denies thousands of
applicants each year who could have done the work well — that is arithmetic, not judgement.
Build a list where the top few are the lottery and the rest are schools you would genuinely
be happy at, and put the same care into those applications. The work in this document makes
you a better candidate everywhere, which is the only part of it you control.
