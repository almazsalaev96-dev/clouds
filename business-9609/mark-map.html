The mark arithmetic

The A Level is 200 raw marks across four papers. Weighting equals raw marks ÷ 2 for every single paper — so every raw mark is worth exactly the same, everywhere. No paper deserves disproportionate panic.

Only 50 of 200 marks reward knowing things.

AO1 is the smallest quarter — and it is the one almost every candidate spends eighty per cent of their revision on. Memorising the syllabus perfectly, and doing nothing else, caps you at roughly a C or B. This is the single most common reason capable students miss A*.

Paper 4 is the densest lever in the qualification

Seventy-five per cent of Paper 4 is analysis and evaluation. It is only two essays. The published weightings force this split for each 20-mark essay:

Marks available3287

A beautifully defined, perfectly contextualised essay with no chains of reasoning and no judgement scores 5/20. Examiner reports say precisely this: candidates "develop only limited evaluative sections."

On Paper 1's essay, the split is more knowledge-weighted — roughly AO1 4 / AO2 4 / AO3 6 / AO4 6. Treat that as an estimate; the Paper 4 figures above are forced arithmetically by the published weightings.

The rule: when a question's time is up, stop mid-sentence. The last five marks of an over-running question are the hardest marks on the paper; the first five marks of the next question are the easiest. Abandoning on time is worth more than anything you know.

02

What examiners actually reward

AO2 — naming the business is not application

Examiner reports are explicit: repeating the case-study name without specific reference does not count. Application means using something only true of this business — a figure from the case, its market conditions, its stated objective, its constraint, its ownership or stage.

No marks

"This would help Zeta Ltd increase its sales and improve profitability."

Application

"With only $40 000 of retained profit and no access to share capital as a family-owned business, Zeta cannot fund this from internal sources."

The test: cover the business name. If the sentence still works for any random company, it isn't application. Rewrite it.

AO3 — build chains, not lists

A point is worth nothing; a chain is worth marks. Minimum three links: cause → mechanism → effect → consequence for this business.

A list

"Delegation improves motivation."

A chain

"Delegating stock ordering to branch managers gives them control over a decision that visibly affects their branch, which satisfies the esteem needs Maslow places above security — and because the case reports 34% turnover concentrated in supervisory grades, retaining them directly cuts the $18 000 recruitment cost."

Words that signal a chain: which means · so · therefore · as a result · this leads to · consequently · which in turn. A paragraph containing none of them contains no analysis.

AO4 — the eight evaluation moves

This is where A* is won. Generic conclusions score near zero. Deploy two or three of these per essay, always tied to case specifics.

  1. 01Depends-onName the variable the whole answer hinges on."This holds only if demand is price-inelastic; the 15% sales drop after a 5% price rise suggests it isn't."
  2. 02Short run versus long runA decision that hurts now and pays later, or the reverse.
  3. 03Relative significanceOf the factors discussed, which matters most, and why. Ranking is evaluation; listing is not.
  4. 04Stakeholder conflictWho gains, who loses, and whose claim is stronger here.
  5. 05Quality of the evidenceSample size, age of the data, opinion presented as fact, forecast assumptions. High-value — it earns AO3 credit too.
  6. 06Opportunity costWhat else could that money, capacity or management time do?
  7. 07Risk and reversibilityHow bad if it fails — and can it be undone?
  8. 08Assumption challengeThe theory assumes X; in this business, X does not hold.

The conclusion that earns full AO4

A top conclusion is not a summary. It commits to an answer, explains why one factor outweighed the others, and attaches a condition drawn from the case.

On balance, firm should decision, because the decisive factor — which here outweighs the strongest counter-argument, since case-specific reason. This holds provided condition from the case; if that fails, alternative becomes preferable.

Four sentences. Reliably worth the top evaluation band. Never write "both options have their merits."

Command words set a ceiling

Recommend · AdviseAO4Must end with a specific, justified course of action

The highest-frequency avoidable error in this subject: writing evaluation into an "Analyse" question, where it earns nothing, and writing none into an "Evaluate" question, where it is most of the marks.

Calculation traps flagged in examiner reports

  • Inverting the formula — profit ÷ revenue, not revenue ÷ profit
  • Forgetting to multiply by 100 — a ratio is not a percentage
  • Mark-up is not margin — a 50% mark-up on cost is a 33.3% profit margin
  • Always state the unit: $ · % · days · times
  • Always add one sentence saying what the number means — that is often where the mark sits

Presentation. One paragraph per point. Examiner reports state directly that separate paragraphs greatly increase the chance each point is recognised. Wall-of-text answers lose marks that were genuinely earned.

04

The learning science

Method matters more than hours. Everything here is a replicated finding in cognitive psychology, mapped to a concrete 9609 action.

Retrieval practice — the testing effect

Recalling information strengthens memory far more than re-reading it. Action: never read a topic without immediately closing the page and writing the whole thing from blank paper, then correcting in a second colour.

Spaced repetition

Material revisited at expanding intervals is retained far longer than material studied in one block. Action: every topic gets a ten-minute blank-paper recall on day 1, 3, 7, 16 and 35. Each touch is a recall, never a re-read.

Interleaving

Mixing topics beats studying them in blocks. It feels harder and worse, and it produces better exam performance. Action: mix finance, motivation and marketing in one sitting — Papers 2, 3 and 4 jump across the syllabus inside a single case study, so train for that.

The fluency illusion is your main enemy.

Highlighting, re-reading and watching videos create a feeling of mastery that does not survive an exam hall. Recognising material is mistaken for being able to produce it. The only valid evidence you know something is closed-book output that you then mark against a real mark scheme. Ban the highlighter.

Five amplifiers

  • Elaborative interrogation. For every concept ask "why does this work, and when does it fail?" The second half is literally your AO4 bank.
  • The generation effect. Attempt the answer before reading the mark scheme, even when you're sure you'll fail. The struggle is what makes it stick.
  • Pre-testing. Attempt questions on topics you haven't studied yet — the failed attempt primes the later learning.
  • Delayed feedback. Mark your own paper the next day, not immediately. Delayed correction retains better.
  • Deliberate practice. Don't "revise Business." If your evaluation is weak, spend a week writing only conclusions — twenty of them, no essays. Isolate, drill, reintegrate.

Exam-day psychology

  • Expressive writing. Ten minutes writing about your exam worries shortly beforehand has been shown to significantly raise scores in anxious students (Ramirez & Beilock, Science, 2011). It frees the working memory anxiety was consuming. Do it in the corridor.
  • Stress reappraisal. Relabel arousal as fuel — "my heart is racing because my body is getting ready" beats trying to calm down. Suppression costs working memory; reappraisal doesn't.
  • Implementation intentions. Pre-decide with if–then rules: if I don't know the answer, then I write the definition and move on; if thirty minutes have passed on essay one, then I start essay two regardless. Pre-committed rules survive panic. Good intentions don't.
  • Sleep. Consolidation happens during sleep — it is the mechanism by which yesterday's revision becomes memory. A five-hour night costs more marks than five hours of cramming gains.
  • The first five minutes. Read the whole paper before writing, and start with the question you can answer best.
05

The plan

Phases scale to whatever time remains — the percentages work whether you have three weeks or nine months.

0one sitting

Diagnostic

Sit one full past paper cold, timed and closed-book — before revising anything. Mark it yourself against the official mark scheme and tally your marks by AO. You now know whether your problem is content, application, chains or judgement. Almost everyone discovers it's AO3/AO4 — and almost everyone would otherwise have spent the term fixing AO1.

1~25%

Content spine

One handwritten page per topic: definition · mechanism · one framework or formula · two lines on when it fails · one real example. Each page written from memory first, then corrected against the textbook in another colour. The correction pass is the learning.

2~35%

Skill drilling — the A* phase

Separated from content deliberately, because AO3 and AO4 are skills that transfer across topics.

  • Chain drills — write a three-link causal chain into a given context. Twenty a week, five minutes each.
  • Evaluation drills — take a past-paper essay title and write only the conclusion, using the four-sentence template. Ten a week.
  • Application drills — rewrite one generic sentence three ways for three different case-study businesses.
  • Calculation sets — every formula until automatic, including the interpreting sentence.
3~30%

Timed papers and self-marking

Full papers under real time, no notes. Mark them yourself the next day against official mark schemes — this is the highest-value activity in the subject, because it teaches you the marker's model of a good answer, which is the actual object of assessment. Keep an error log: every lost mark tagged by AO and by cause. Patterns appear within three papers. Work backwards through recent series, then across all variants.

4final ~10%

Taper

No new content. Spaced recall of the one-page spine only. One paper every other day, not every day. Rehearse the eight evaluation moves and the conclusion template until reflexive. Sleep, timing rules, if–then plans.

06

The checklist

Run this against every essay you write between now and the exam.

  • Have I identified the command word and the AO ceiling it sets?
  • Is every point in its own paragraph?
  • Does every paragraph contain which means / so / therefore — a chain, not a list?
  • Is there something in every paragraph only true of this business — a number, a constraint, an objective, a market condition?
  • Have I used two or three named evaluation moves?
  • Does my conclusion commit to an answer, say why that factor outweighed the others, and attach a condition?
  • Did I finish on time?

Seven boxes. They are, almost exactly, the mark scheme.
